Circus · a valley city

Question (user, 26 October): A, B and C don't make sense as transport networks. How might a Chongqing-style landscape work, with a topography that explains the levels, and some city-planning logic round the circus?

Answer in one line: the ground moves, the roads don't. Hills are Z1 ground; the valley is Z0. A road on a hill is a street; where the valley opens under it, the same road stands on columns. The circus sits on the valley floor because that is where the valley road meets the only roads up the hills.

The logic

  1. Roads keep their level; the ground moves. A hill is Z1 ground, the valley Z0. A Z1 road is a street on the hill and a bridge over the valley.
  2. Every road goes somewhere. Each runs edge to edge or ends at a junction. No loops for their own sake.
  3. Roads follow the terrain. The arterial runs along the valley floor. Ridge roads run along the hilltops. Bridges link hill to hill across the valley.
  4. Levels meet where the terrain allows. Roads climb in coves (side valleys) where the floor ramps up. People climb by public escalators and through buildings with doors on both levels.
  5. The circus is a real junction. It is where the valley avenue meets both climbs: the one place valley traffic can turn uphill.

    Chongqing, for reference

    • Hongya Cave: 11 floors against a cliff; one entrance on the upper road, one by the river.
