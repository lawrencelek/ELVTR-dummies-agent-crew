Circus · a valley city by the river

Question (user, 26 October): A, B and C don't make sense as transport networks. How might a Chongqing-style landscape work, with a topography that explains the levels, and some city-planning logic round the circus?

Answer in one line: the ground moves, the roads don't. The hill is Z1 ground; the valley and beach are Z0; the river lies below the seawall. A road on the hill is a street; where the ground falls away under it, the same road stands on columns. The circus sits on the valley floor where the avenue meets the climb up the hill and the road down to the river.

Edit 59 (user, 26 October): the south side is a riverbank. Row 2 runs cliff, avenue, beach. The two Z1 roads go straight south over the river; a coastal drive at Z0 winds along the seawall, horizontal, 45°, horizontal, under both.

The logic

  1. Roads keep their level; the ground moves. A hill is Z1 ground, the valley Z0. A Z1 road is a street on the hill and a bridge over the valley.
  2. Every road goes somewhere. Each runs edge to edge or ends at a junction. No loops for their own sake.
  3. Roads follow the terrain. The arterial runs along the valley floor; the ridge road along the hilltop; the coastal drive along the shore. The bridges carry the hill's traffic straight over the valley and the river.
  4. Levels meet where the terrain allows. Roads climb in coves (side valleys) where the floor ramps up. People climb by public escalators and through buildings with doors on both levels.
  5. The circus is a real junction. The valley avenue meets the climb up the hill and the road down to the river: the one place the valley turns uphill or to the water.
